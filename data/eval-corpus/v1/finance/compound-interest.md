# Compound Interest — Math, Time Horizon Impact

Compound interest is the engine of long-term wealth accumulation. Understanding why it matters and how it interacts with time horizon clarifies why early retirement-account contributions outperform later, larger ones.

## The math

The compound interest formula for periodic compounding is:

    A = P (1 + r/n)^(nt)

Where A is the future value, P is the principal, r is the annual interest rate, n is the number of compounding periods per year, and t is years. For continuous compounding, the formula collapses to A = Pe^(rt).

The key insight is the exponential `t` — doubling time doesn't merely double the result. At 7% annual return (a reasonable real-return assumption for diversified equities), $1 invested becomes:

| Years | Multiplier |
|---|---|
| 10 | 2.0× |
| 20 | 3.9× |
| 30 | 7.6× |
| 40 | 15.0× |
| 50 | 29.5× |

A dollar invested at age 25 produces seven times more at age 65 than a dollar invested at age 45. This is why the conventional advice — start early, even with small amounts — compounds (literally) into outsized lifetime outcomes.

## The Rule of 72

A useful shortcut: years to double ≈ 72 / annual return percent. At 7%, money doubles every ~10 years. At 10%, every 7 years. At 4%, every 18 years. The rule is exact for continuous compounding and approximate for periodic compounding, but accurate enough for mental math.

## Real vs nominal returns

The 7% number assumes real returns — after inflation. Nominal returns on US equities have averaged ~10% over the past century, but inflation averaged ~3%, so real (purchasing-power) growth is closer to 7%. Always plan in real terms when projecting decades out, because future dollars buy less.

For shorter horizons, nominal returns matter for nominal liabilities (paying off a fixed-rate mortgage, for instance). For lifetime planning, real returns are the honest framework.

## Time horizon and risk tolerance

A 30-year horizon can accept a 50% drawdown without panic because the compounding math says even a sustained bear market is a setback that recovers within the planning horizon. A 5-year horizon cannot — the same drawdown in year 4 means selling at a loss to fund a planned expense.

This is why financial planning frameworks shift asset allocation toward bonds as withdrawal nears. The age-based "100 minus age in stocks" or "110 minus age" rules are crude but capture the directionally-correct shift: early career = mostly equities (long horizon, can ride out volatility); near retirement = mix of bonds and equities (need stability for upcoming withdrawals while still benefiting from continued growth).

## Concrete example

A 25-year-old contributing $500/month to a Roth IRA at 7% real return reaches $1.2M at age 65. The same $500/month started at age 35 reaches only $570K. The same $500/month started at age 45 reaches only $250K.

Same monthly contribution, three radically different outcomes. The differential is purely time. This is why the second-best time to start saving is today — the very best time was 10 years ago.

## When compound interest works against you

Credit-card debt at 22% APR compounds the other direction. $5,000 of credit-card debt at minimum payments (typically 2-3% of balance) takes decades to pay off and balloons to multiples of the original. Eliminating high-interest debt is mathematically equivalent to a guaranteed return at the debt's interest rate — usually a far better "investment" than equities.

The hierarchy: pay off any debt above ~7-8% before contributing beyond employer-match retirement. Below that threshold, the math leans toward investing while paying minimums.
