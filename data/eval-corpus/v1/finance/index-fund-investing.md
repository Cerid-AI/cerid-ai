# Index Fund Investing — Total Market, Expense Ratios, Tax Efficiency

Index funds are pooled investment vehicles that track a market benchmark — most commonly the total US stock market, S&P 500, or a global equity index. The fund holds securities in proportion to the benchmark, accepting market returns rather than attempting to beat them.

## Why total-market over narrower indexes

A total-market fund (e.g., VTI, FZROX) holds essentially every publicly traded US stock weighted by market capitalization. This includes large-cap, mid-cap, and small-cap stocks across all sectors. Total-market beats S&P 500 (large-cap only) on diversification but the two correlate at ~0.99 over multi-year horizons because S&P 500 represents most of the market by capitalization.

The case for total-market is theoretical: in efficient markets, you cannot reliably identify which segment will outperform. By holding the whole market, you guarantee market returns minus your costs. The case for S&P 500 is liquidity and tax-loss harvesting partner availability — for most retail investors, the practical difference is small.

## Expense ratios

The expense ratio is the annual fee a fund charges, expressed as a percentage of assets. A 0.03% expense ratio on a $10,000 position costs $3/year. Compounded over decades, low expense ratios are the single biggest controllable factor in long-term returns.

Vanguard, Fidelity, Schwab, and iShares all offer total-market and S&P 500 funds at expense ratios under 0.05%. Active funds — those trying to beat the market — typically charge 0.5% to 1.5%. Over 30 years, a 1% fee differential on a $100,000 portfolio compounds to roughly $230,000 in foregone returns at 7% nominal growth.

Some Fidelity zero-fee funds (FZROX, FZILX) charge 0% — they make money on securities lending and order routing. Tax cost is identical to other index funds in tax-advantaged accounts; in taxable accounts, the funds are not portable to other brokerages, which can lock you in.

## Tax efficiency

Index funds are inherently tax-efficient compared to active funds because they trade infrequently — only when the underlying index rebalances. Low turnover means few realized capital gains distributed to shareholders.

ETF wrappers add another layer of efficiency through the in-kind creation/redemption mechanism: when an authorized participant redeems shares, they receive securities rather than cash, which avoids forcing the fund to sell appreciated holdings. Mutual fund index funds rebalance internally without this mechanism, occasionally distributing capital gains.

For taxable accounts, prefer ETFs (VTI over VTSAX) for tax efficiency. For tax-advantaged accounts (401(k), IRA), the wrapper doesn't matter — both work equally well.

## Asset allocation context

A total-market index fund is typically 60-100% of the equity portion of a long-term portfolio. International diversification (VXUS or similar) covers the 40-50% of global market cap outside the US. Bond funds (BND) provide stability for shorter horizons. The right mix depends on time horizon and risk tolerance — younger investors with decades of contributions ahead can stomach more volatility than those nearing withdrawal.

A simple three-fund portfolio (US total market, international, bonds) at age-appropriate ratios captures the dominant share of academic finance's recommendations with near-zero ongoing maintenance.
