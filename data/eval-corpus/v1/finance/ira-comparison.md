# Roth IRA vs Traditional IRA — Tax Treatment, Contribution Limits

Individual Retirement Accounts (IRAs) come in two main flavors that differ on when you pay tax. Choosing between them depends on your current vs expected future tax bracket, time horizon, and other tax-planning levers.

## Traditional IRA

Contributions to a traditional IRA are tax-deductible in the year you make them (subject to income limits and workplace-retirement-plan participation). The investments grow tax-deferred. When you withdraw in retirement, the entire withdrawal — original contribution plus growth — is taxed as ordinary income.

The deductibility phase-out applies when you (or your spouse) are covered by a workplace retirement plan (401(k), 403(b)) and your income exceeds the IRS thresholds. For 2025, single filers covered by a workplace plan see deduction phase-out between $77,000 and $87,000 modified AGI; married filing jointly with both spouses covered is $123,000-$143,000.

Required Minimum Distributions (RMDs) begin at age 73 (rising to 75 in 2033 per SECURE 2.0). The RMD forces withdrawal of a percentage of the balance each year, generating taxable income whether you need it or not.

## Roth IRA

Roth contributions are made with post-tax dollars — no current-year deduction. The investments grow tax-free, and qualified withdrawals (after age 59½ and 5 years from the first contribution) are entirely tax-free, including all growth.

Roth contributions have direct income limits, not phase-out from workplace coverage. For 2025, single filers can contribute fully under $146,000 modified AGI, with phase-out to $161,000. Married filing jointly is $230,000-$240,000.

There are no RMDs on Roth IRAs during the original owner's lifetime — the account can grow tax-free indefinitely until the owner draws it down or passes it to heirs.

## Contribution limits (2025)

The annual contribution limit applies to traditional and Roth IRAs combined: $7,000 for those under 50, $8,000 for those 50 and over (catch-up contribution). You can split between both accounts in the same year as long as the combined total stays within the limit.

These limits are separate from 401(k) limits — you can max out both if income permits.

## Choosing between them

The core decision is whether your current marginal tax rate is higher or lower than your expected retirement marginal rate.

- **Traditional preferred** when current rate > retirement rate. Deduct now at the higher rate, pay later at the lower rate.
- **Roth preferred** when current rate < retirement rate. Pay tax now at the lower rate; tax-free growth is more valuable.

Younger workers in their 20s and 30s usually expect higher rates later (career progression, possibly higher future tax rates). Roth tends to win for them. Mid-career workers in peak earning years often benefit from traditional. Retirees in low-income years can do strategic Roth conversions.

Other Roth advantages: contributions (not earnings) can be withdrawn anytime tax-free and penalty-free, making Roth IRAs more flexible as a backup emergency fund. Roth heirs inherit tax-free distributions over a 10-year window.

## Backdoor Roth IRA

For high earners over the Roth income limit: contribute to a non-deductible traditional IRA, then convert to Roth in the same year. The pro-rata rule complicates this if you have other pre-tax IRA balances — consult a tax professional before executing.

## Where they fit in the priority order

A common contribution priority for someone with access to all options: 401(k) up to the employer match → max HSA if eligible → max IRA (Roth or traditional based on the above) → return to 401(k) up to its annual limit → taxable brokerage. The exact order depends on individual fee structures and tax situation.
