# Reading Habits — Book Selection, Retention Strategies

People who read a lot read faster, retain more, and find books that compound on their existing knowledge. The skills behind this aren't innate; they're built through deliberate practice on a few specific habits.

## Book selection

The single biggest leverage point in reading is choosing the right book. A great book read poorly outperforms a mediocre book read carefully. Heuristics that help:

- **Skim before committing.** Read the table of contents, the introduction, and the first chapter before deciding to invest. Most non-fiction books contain three valuable chapters and seven that pad the publisher's word count requirement.
- **Trace recommendations from people whose taste you trust** — not bestseller lists. A book repeatedly cited by three authors you respect is more reliable than a book topping Amazon's charts this week.
- **Prefer older books** that have outlasted their initial promotional cycle. A book still being read 20 years after publication has been filtered for durable value; a book published last quarter has not.
- **Match the book to the question you're trying to answer.** Reading is fastest when you're hungry for what's on the page. Ambiently-collected unread books rarely get read; specifically-needed books usually do.

The corollary: it's fine to abandon books. The sunk cost of half-read books is a fallacy; the opportunity cost of finishing a book you've stopped enjoying is real. Tim Urban's heuristic — "if you wouldn't recommend it to a friend at this point, stop reading" — is a useful filter at the 25% mark.

## Active vs passive reading

Passive reading — moving your eyes over the page — leaves about 10% retention by next week. Active reading — generating connections, examples, questions, and disagreements as you go — can push that to 40-60%.

The cheapest form of active reading: write in the margins. Question marks where the argument is unclear, exclamation points at insights, brief paraphrases at the top of dense paragraphs. The act of writing — even a few words — forces engagement.

For higher commitment: maintain notes. Sönke Ahrens's *How to Take Smart Notes* describes the "literature note → permanent note" pipeline — capturing the author's claim in your own words, then writing standalone notes that connect ideas across books over time. The Zettelkasten system formalizes this; index cards or tools like Obsidian, Roam, or LogSeq mechanize it.

## Retention strategies

Beyond active reading during the book, three practices boost long-term retention:

1. **Spaced retrieval.** Days or weeks after finishing, prompt yourself to recall the book's core ideas without looking. Tools like Readwise automate this with periodic emails of your highlights. The act of retrieval (not re-reading) strengthens memory.

2. **Talk about it.** Explaining a book to someone else — informally or in a structured book club — surfaces what you remember vs what you only thought you understood. The Feynman technique formalizes this: explain the concept simply, identify gaps, return to the source.

3. **Apply it.** A book whose ideas inform a real decision lodges deeper than one merely admired. Picking one specific concept to test in your work or life within a week of finishing is more valuable than reading a dozen more books.

## Reading speed

Speed reading marketed by certain courses (1000 wpm with comprehension) is largely myth — comprehension drops sharply above ~400 wpm for difficult material. But average adult reading speed (~250 wpm) does have headroom. The cheap improvements:

- **Reduce subvocalization** — the inner voice reading along — for material that doesn't require careful sentence-level parsing
- **Skim non-essential sections** — chapters whose ideas are familiar, examples that elaborate a known point
- **Vary speed by section** — slow on the dense pages, fast on the connectors

The deepest material rewards slow careful reading; the journalistic non-fiction that fills most adult reading rewards skimming.

## Building the habit

The habits that sustain heavy reading:
- **Always have a book in progress.** Friction-free transitions to the next session.
- **Default to reading in transition moments** — before sleep, on transit, during waits. Replaces phone scrolling.
- **Track what you read.** A simple list with month and brief reflection produces over-time perspective on what's been valuable.
- **Read multiple books concurrently** if attention varies — fiction for evenings, denser non-fiction for mornings, lighter craft books in fragmented moments.

Volume matters less than consistency. A person reading 30 minutes per day finishes 25-30 books per year; that's transformative over a decade. Heroic monthly bursts followed by months of nothing typically don't produce comparable depth.
