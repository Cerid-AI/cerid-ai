# Sleep Hygiene — Circadian Rhythm, Screen Time

Sleep quality is upstream of nearly every cognitive and physical performance metric. The interventions that consistently move the needle are unsexy, well-replicated, and largely free.

## Circadian rhythm — the master clock

The body's circadian rhythm is a roughly 24-hour cycle regulating sleep, wakefulness, body temperature, hormone release, and many other functions. The suprachiasmatic nucleus in the hypothalamus synchronizes this clock primarily through light exposure to the retina — bright light in the morning advances the clock; bright light in the evening delays it.

Two practical implications:

1. **Morning light exposure** within an hour of waking — direct sunlight is best, even on overcast days; bright (10,000 lux) light therapy boxes work for those without sun access. 10-15 minutes is typically sufficient. This anchors the wake side of the cycle and predicts the sleep side ~16 hours later.

2. **Dim evening light** for 2-3 hours before bed. Bright overhead lights, screens, and especially blue-spectrum light suppress melatonin (the hormone that signals sleep readiness). Even moderate evening light exposure can delay melatonin onset by 1-2 hours, postponing sleep accordingly.

A consistent sleep-wake schedule — same bedtime and wake time within ±30 minutes seven days a week — strengthens the circadian signal more than any single intervention. Weekend "catch-up" sleep that shifts wake time by 2+ hours produces social jet lag with measurable Monday-morning impact.

## Screen time and blue light

Phones, laptops, tablets, and TVs emit substantial blue-spectrum light at the eye level when held close. The effect on melatonin is well-replicated; the magnitude in individual users is variable.

Effective mitigations, in order of strength:
- **Stop using screens 1-2 hours before bed.** The most effective intervention; rarely the easiest.
- **Use system-level night-mode settings** (iOS Night Shift, Android Night Light, macOS Night Shift, f.lux) — they shift the spectrum away from blue. Effect on melatonin is real but smaller than expected.
- **Wear blue-blocking glasses** in the evening — amber or red lenses for 2 hours pre-bed. More effective than night-mode software, more clinical-feeling.
- **Set screen brightness low** in the evening. Compounds with the above.

The behavioral content of evening screen use matters as much as the light itself. Doomscrolling triggers cortisol; suspenseful media activates the sympathetic nervous system. The adjustments that work tend to be both spectral (less blue) and behavioral (less arousing content).

## Sleep environment

Three factors dominate:

- **Temperature** — the body cools as it falls asleep; ambient temperatures of 60-67°F (16-19°C) support this. Warmer rooms produce shallower, more fragmented sleep.
- **Darkness** — even small amounts of light penetrating eyelids during sleep elevate cortisol the next day. Blackout curtains, sleep masks, or blocking LED indicators on electronics all compound.
- **Quiet** — sustained background noise above ~30 dB disrupts sleep architecture. Earplugs, white noise machines, or fans (which provide both sound masking and cooling) help.

A useful diagnostic: if you wake during the night feeling hot, the room is too warm; if you wake to ambient sound, isolation is insufficient.

## Caffeine and alcohol

Caffeine has a half-life of 5-7 hours in most adults. Coffee at 2pm leaves substantial caffeine in the bloodstream at 10pm bedtime. The effect on sleep onset varies widely person-to-person (genetic differences in CYP1A2 metabolism), but the safe rule is no caffeine after early afternoon.

Alcohol is sedating but disrupts sleep architecture — particularly REM sleep in the second half of the night. A single drink before bed measurably degrades sleep quality even when you fall asleep faster. Higher doses produce wake-ups in the early-morning hours from acetaldehyde metabolism.

## When to seek help

Persistent insomnia (3+ months of difficulty falling or staying asleep, with daytime impact) warrants Cognitive Behavioral Therapy for Insomnia (CBT-I) — first-line treatment per medical guidelines, more effective than medications and without the dependence risk. Sleep medications (Z-drugs, Ambien, etc.) are tactical aids, not solutions.

Suspected sleep apnea (loud snoring, witnessed pauses, daytime sleepiness despite adequate time in bed) warrants a sleep study. CPAP and other treatments dramatically improve quality for affected people; the condition is dangerously common and underdiagnosed.

## The cost-benefit

Sleep is the highest-leverage intervention for cognitive performance, mood regulation, and long-term health. The interventions above cost essentially nothing financially and modestly in evening flexibility. The return — measured in next-day energy, focus, decision quality, and disease risk over decades — is disproportionate. People who treat sleep as optional discover otherwise eventually, often expensively.
