# Time Blocking — Calendar Discipline, Deep Work

Time blocking schedules specific work into specific calendar slots ahead of time, treating the calendar as a contract rather than a list of meetings. Cal Newport's *Deep Work* and Nir Eyal's *Indistractable* are common starting points; the practice predates both.

## The core idea

Without time blocking, the typical knowledge worker's day flows reactively: meetings on the calendar, email/Slack throughout, and "real work" squeezed into the gaps. The day ends with shallow accomplishment — many things touched, little of substance moved.

With time blocking, you allocate every working hour in advance to either:
- **A scheduled meeting** (the calendar's traditional content)
- **A specific task or category of work** (e.g., 9-11am: write Q3 plan; 11-12: email; 2-4pm: code review the search refactor)
- **Deep work** — a multi-hour block protected from interruption for cognitively demanding tasks

The discipline is two-fold: actually doing what the block says, and resisting the urge to context-switch when an interruption appears.

## Deep work blocks

Deep work — Newport's term for focused, undistracted cognitive labor — produces disproportionate output for knowledge workers. Writing, design, debugging difficult problems, learning new domains all benefit from extended uninterrupted focus.

The mechanics that make deep work blocks succeed:
- **Multi-hour duration** — context-switching costs are nontrivial; sub-1-hour blocks rarely reach the productive zone for hard work.
- **Explicit communication** — let collaborators know you'll be unreachable during the block, with a defined check-in time after.
- **Notifications off** — Slack closed or in DnD mode, email closed, phone in another room.
- **Defined exit** — the block ends at a specific time; this constraint sharpens focus rather than letting work expand to fill all available time.

Most knowledge workers can sustain 2-4 hours of true deep work per day; pushing for more leads to diminishing returns and burnout. Schedule deep work for your peak cognitive hours (often morning for many people).

## Shallow work blocks

Shallow work — email, Slack, administrative tasks, quick follow-ups — fills the rest of the day. Time blocking shallow work prevents the inverse problem: shallow work expanding to consume time that should be deep.

A common pattern: 30 minutes mid-morning and 30 minutes mid-afternoon for "communications", with email/Slack closed otherwise. The friction of waiting until the next block tends to dissolve a surprising amount of "urgent" requests that resolve themselves.

## The weekly reset

Time blocking works at two levels:
- **Weekly planning** (typically Friday afternoon or Sunday) — review the upcoming week, commit to outcomes, slot major work into the calendar
- **Daily review** (10 minutes at start of day) — adjust the day's blocks based on yesterday's overflow and any new information

Without the weekly view, daily blocks become tactical noise. Without the daily review, the week's plan stops matching reality by Tuesday.

## Resistance and adjustments

The blocks will be wrong. A 2-hour estimate becomes 4 hours; a meeting runs over; an urgent issue derails the morning. The point isn't that the original plan was perfect — it's that you make explicit decisions to deviate rather than drifting.

A useful practice: at the end of each day, look at planned vs actual. Repeated overruns of certain task types reveal estimation bias to correct in next week's planning.

## When time blocking fails

- **Calendar density too high** — if 80%+ of the week is meetings, time blocking can't help; the upstream problem is meeting culture, not personal discipline.
- **Reactive role** — incident responders, support engineers, sales leaders living on inbound calls can't time-block in the same way. Smaller blocks (30 min) interleaved with reactive availability work better.
- **Perfectionism in adherence** — treating every deviation as a failure burns out the practice within weeks.

## Tools

The tool matters less than the practice. Calendar apps (Google Calendar, Outlook, Fantastical) work fine; specialized apps (Reclaim.ai, Sunsama, Motion) automate some of the planning. The minimum useful setup is a calendar with clear event titles for non-meeting blocks.

For people who keep a separate task list, link the day's blocks to specific tasks — "9-11am: write Q3 plan" with the doc bookmarked. The handoff from "what should I do next?" to "what did I commit to do now?" is where blocking earns its overhead.
