# Exercise Routine — Cardio + Strength Balance

A well-designed exercise routine for the general adult balances cardiovascular work, strength training, and mobility/recovery. The American Heart Association and CDC guidelines codify the targets; adherence over years matters more than program optimality.

## The minimum effective dose

Public health guidelines for adults:

- **150 minutes per week of moderate-intensity aerobic activity** OR 75 minutes of vigorous-intensity, or an equivalent combination
- **Strength training 2+ days per week** covering all major muscle groups
- **Flexibility/mobility work** 2-3 days per week, particularly for older adults

These are floors, not ceilings. Substantially more produces additional benefits with diminishing returns; the sweet spot for most general-health gains is ~300 minutes/week of cardio with 3-4 strength sessions.

## Cardiovascular training

Cardio improvements come from accumulated time in the right intensity zones:

- **Zone 2 (60-70% max heart rate)** — sustainable conversational pace. Builds aerobic base and mitochondrial density. Should be the bulk of weekly cardio time. Examples: brisk walking, easy cycling, slow jogging.
- **Zone 4-5 (HIIT, 85-95% max HR)** — short intervals of high effort with rest periods. Improves VO₂ max, the strongest predictor of all-cause mortality among modifiable factors. 1-2 sessions per week of 15-30 minutes is plenty.
- **Zone 3 (the "junk miles")** — the gray zone too hard for full recovery between sessions but not hard enough to drive aerobic adaptation. Easy to accidentally live in; produces fatigue without proportionate gain.

A practical week for someone with no extreme training goals: three 45-60 minute Zone 2 sessions, one short HIIT workout, and the rest recovery / strength.

## Strength training

The strongest evidence supports compound lifts that train multiple muscle groups through full ranges of motion: squats, deadlifts, presses (overhead and bench), rows, and pull-ups. These lifts build relative strength efficiently and stimulate the hormonal responses that drive broader adaptation.

A minimal effective program:
- **3 sessions per week** (e.g., Monday, Wednesday, Friday) with rest days between for recovery
- **2-3 compound lifts per session**, 3-5 sets of 5-10 reps
- **Progressive overload** — add weight, reps, or sets weekly when possible

Beginners gain rapidly on simple linear progression (Starting Strength, Stronglifts 5×5). Intermediates plateau and benefit from periodized programs (5/3/1, GZCL templates). Advanced lifters need individualized programming.

For older adults (50+), strength training is particularly important — it preserves muscle mass against sarcopenia, maintains bone density against osteoporosis, and reduces fall risk. The interventions that maintain function into the 70s and 80s are mostly lifting, not cardio.

## Recovery and progression

Adaptation happens during rest, not during workouts. Inadequate recovery produces accumulated fatigue, plateaus, and injury risk:

- **Sleep** — the highest-leverage recovery factor. 7-9 hours, with consistent timing.
- **Nutrition** — adequate protein (0.7-1.0g per pound of bodyweight for active adults), enough calories to support training load, sufficient carbohydrate for the intensity of work performed.
- **Deload weeks** every 4-8 weeks reduce volume by 30-50% to consolidate gains and prevent burnout.

Progression follows a stair-step pattern: load increases for several weeks, plateaus, then a deload sets up another increase. Trying to add weight every session indefinitely produces injury more than progress past the beginner phase.

## Common pitfalls

- **Cardio-only programs** miss the strength contribution to longevity, metabolic health, and quality of life in later decades
- **Strength-only programs** miss the cardiovascular benefits that dominate cardiovascular and all-cause mortality reduction
- **Excessive intensity** without recovery — high-stress workouts daily produce overreaching, then overtraining
- **Program-hopping** — abandoning a program after 2-3 weeks rather than seeing it through. Most programs work; consistency does most of the work
- **Ignoring mobility** — tightness from sedentary work and weighted compound lifts compounds; 10 minutes of mobility work post-session pays back in injury avoidance

## Building the habit

The exercise routines that survive years tend to share characteristics:
- **Scheduled at consistent times** rather than slotted opportunistically
- **Locationally easy** — home gym, gym on commute, neighborhood loop. Friction kills adherence faster than motivation gaps.
- **Tracked** — a simple log of what was done provides progression data and accountability
- **Tied to identity** — "I'm a runner" or "I lift" becomes self-reinforcing in a way that "I'm trying to get healthy" doesn't

Most people who succeed at long-term exercise have miss-and-resume cycles measured in days, not months. Skipping a week is normal; treating it as failure and abandoning the habit isn't.
