# Critical Thinking — Cognitive Biases, Evidence Weighing

Critical thinking is the practice of evaluating claims and evidence with awareness of the systematic ways human reasoning fails. The goal isn't certainty — it's calibrated confidence and resistance to common traps.

## The most-cited biases (and what to do about them)

**Confirmation bias** — selectively noticing and remembering evidence that supports existing beliefs while overlooking contradicting evidence. The defense is structural: actively seek the strongest counterargument before concluding. Steel-man, then critique.

**Anchoring** — first information disproportionately influences subsequent judgment. Initial price quotes, opening offers, first impressions all anchor downstream evaluation. Defense: write down your independent estimate before exposure to others' anchors; use formal multi-stage estimation (e.g., wisdom-of-crowds methods) for important calls.

**Availability heuristic** — judging probability by how easily examples come to mind. Plane crashes feel more common than they are because they're vividly reported; car crashes feel less common despite being thousands of times more frequent. Defense: when probability matters, check actual base rates rather than relying on intuition.

**Sunk cost fallacy** — continuing investment in a failing endeavor because of past expenditure. The past money/time is gone regardless of future decisions; only future expected value should drive forward choices. Defense: ask "would I start this today knowing what I now know?" If no, the past investment is a sunk cost.

**Recency bias** — overweighting recent events relative to long-term trends. Markets, performance reviews, project assessments all suffer. Defense: extend the time window before drawing conclusions; one quarter of data is rarely a trend.

**Survivorship bias** — drawing lessons only from successes, missing the much larger pool of failures that took the same approach. The startup advice from successful founders frequently fails to replicate. Defense: study failures explicitly; look for the dog that didn't bark.

**Dunning-Kruger** — beginners systematically overestimate their competence; experts underestimate theirs. The defense, paradoxically, is calibration practice: predict your performance on tasks before doing them, track actual outcomes, learn how good your self-assessment is.

## Evaluating evidence

Not all evidence is equal. A useful hierarchy for empirical claims:

1. **Pre-registered randomized controlled trials with replications.** The gold standard for causal claims.
2. **Single RCTs.** Strong evidence but unreplicated; some published findings later fail to replicate.
3. **Observational studies with strong design.** Useful for correlation; can suggest but not establish causation.
4. **Anecdote and case studies.** Useful for hypothesis generation; weak for general claims.
5. **Plausibility arguments.** "This makes sense" is not evidence.

For most everyday questions, you'll be working with weaker evidence than ideal. The honest move is to maintain proportional confidence — strong evidence supports strong claims; weak evidence supports tentative claims.

## Considering the source

A claim's credibility depends partly on its source's track record and incentives:

- **Domain expertise** — does the source have demonstrated competence in this specific area? Generic credentials are weaker than domain-specific track records.
- **Incentives** — what would the source say if they were wrong? If they have a financial or ideological stake in the answer, weight accordingly.
- **Update history** — does the source have a record of changing positions when evidence warrants? People who never update are signaling allegiance to a position rather than truth-seeking.

Source consideration cuts both ways: a credible source isn't infallible, and an incentivized source isn't always wrong. The point is calibration, not dismissal.

## Probabilistic thinking

Most consequential decisions involve uncertainty. Treating questions as binary (will this work? Y/N) loses information that probabilistic framing preserves (60% chance this works given our assumptions).

A productive practice:
- **Forecast specifically** — "70% chance the project ships before Sept 15"
- **Track outcomes** — calibration improves with explicit feedback loops
- **Update on evidence** — write down what would change your mind, then notice when it appears

The Brier score and similar calibration metrics quantify forecast quality over time. Even informal practice — making explicit predictions and reviewing them — improves judgment within months.

## Common discussion failures

Beyond individual reasoning, group discussion has its own pathologies:

- **Premature consensus** — the first speaker anchors the group; quieter members don't disagree. Defense: solicit independent written opinions before discussion.
- **Groupthink** — desire for harmony suppresses dissent. Defense: assign a designated devil's advocate role.
- **Strawmanning** — refuting a weakened version of the opposing view. Defense: require steel-manning before critique.
- **Bailey-and-motte** — defending a strong claim by retreating to a weak one when challenged, then reasserting the strong claim. Defense: pin down which claim is being defended.

The goal isn't winning the discussion — it's ending with better-calibrated beliefs than at the start.

## The meta-skill

Critical thinking isn't a single skill but a habit of mind: noticing when you're confident without warrant, when an argument is suspiciously convenient, when a source is invoking authority instead of evidence. The biases catalogued above are useful labels, not a checklist. The deeper skill is recognizing your own thinking in real-time and slowing down when the territory feels uncertain.
